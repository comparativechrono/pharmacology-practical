Why TLR4?

TLR4 is the receptor that lets our innate immune system detect lipopolysaccharide (LPS, endotoxin) from the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. Over-activation of TLR4 drives the inflammation of sepsis, so it has been a target for drugs such as the LPS antagonist eritoran and the small molecule resatorvid (TAK-242). It is a good example of the four questions structure can answer (from the lecture):

How does shape affect function?

The curved, horseshoe-shaped TLR4 ectodomain – Chapter 3.

How does a protein interact with partners?

TLR4 needs its co-receptor MD-2, and forms a dimer – Chapters 2 & 3.

How do mutations affect function?

The common Asp299Gly / Thr399Ile variants – Chapters 1 & 3.

How do ligands and drugs interact?

LPS in MD-2's pocket, and the antagonist eritoran – Chapter 3.

UniProt: what do we know about a protein?

≈ 25 minWorkbench: UniProt tab

UniProt (uniprot.org) is a worldwide consortium that collects and annotates protein sequences and their functions. Each protein has an entry – like its home page – gathering information from experiments, the literature, analysis software and dozens of other databases.

Two halves of UniProtKB

Far too much sequence data arrives for every entry to be checked by hand, so UniProtKB has two parts: Swiss-Prot – reviewed entries curated by experts (★) – and TrEMBL – unreviewed entries annotated automatically. Every entry has a stable accession (e.g. O00206) and a readable entry name (e.g. TLR4_HUMAN).

What is the accession of human TLR4?

O00206 (entry name TLR4_HUMAN). Note that it starts with the letter O, not a zero.

Why is it important to check the organism and the Reviewed status before you use an entry?

Many proteins share a name across species (TLR4 in cat, horse, cow…) and related proteins appear in the same search (TLR9, TLR5…). Using the wrong entry gives the wrong sequence, residue numbers, variants and structures. Reviewed (Swiss-Prot) entries have been checked by curators against the literature; unreviewed (TrEMBL) entries are annotated automatically and may contain unverified predictions.

1.2 Function

The entry is organised into sections, listed down the left of the entry. Function summarises what the protein does, with references. Gene Ontology (GO) terms describe roles in words that computers can search; the code after each term shows the evidence (e.g. IDA = direct assay, IEA = automatic). Pathway databases such as Reactome place the protein in signalling pathways – e.g. R-HSA-166016, Toll Like Receptor 4 (TLR4) Cascade.

Which bacterial molecule does TLR4 respond to, and which protein does it cooperate with at the plasma membrane?

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from Gram-negative bacteria, together with LY96 – better known as MD-2. (CD14 is also part of the LPS receptor complex.)

Name two adaptor proteins through which TLR4 signals inside the cell. What is the end result of the signal?

MYD88 and TIRAP (also called MAL), with TRAF6 acting downstream. At the plasma membrane this leads to activation of NF-κB, secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and an inflammatory response. After TLR4 is taken into the cell by endocytosis, a MyD88-independent pathway uses TRAM (TICAM2) and TRIF (TICAM1), leading via TBK1 and IRF3 to production of type I interferon.

Which two amino-acid changes make up the TLR4*B allele, and what is their reported effect?

Asp299Gly (D299G) and Thr399Ile (T399I). The allele is associated with a reduced (blunted) response to LPS, e.g. to inhaled LPS.

Mutating proline 714 abolishes binding to which adaptor protein?

MYD88 – P714H abolishes MyD88 binding and the LPS response. The equivalent mutation in mouse TLR4 (Pro712His) is why C3H/HeJ mice do not respond to LPS; it helped identify TLR4 as the LPS receptor (Poltorak et al., 1998).

Name one compound listed under Chemistry that was developed to block TLR4. What happened to it?

Eritoran (E5564) – a synthetic analogue of lipid A that competes with LPS for MD-2. It reached a phase III trial in severe sepsis (ACCESS) but did not reduce 28-day mortality (Opal et al., 2013). Resatorvid (TAK-242) instead binds Cys747 inside the cell, in the TIR domain.

1.6 PTM / processing

After translation, proteins are processed and modified. Proteins with an extracellular domain usually lose a signal peptide, and are often glycosylated and stabilised by disulfide bonds.

Activity 1.6
  1. Open PTM / Processing. Click a green glycosylation pin to see where the sugar is attached in 3D.
How many N-linked glycosylation sites are annotated?

10 (Asn-35, 173, 205, 282, 309, 497, 526, 575, 624 and 630). The PTM note explains that Asn-526 and Asn-575 are needed for TLR4 to reach the cell surface.

Residues 1–23 are a signal peptide. What happens to them, and why might an experimental structure start at residue 24 or later?

The signal peptide directs TLR4 into the ER for the secretory pathway and is then cleaved off, so it is not part of the mature receptor. Structures are made from the mature protein (often a shorter fragment), so they start after the signal peptide – 3FXI starts at residue 27. UniProt keeps numbering from the precursor's Met-1, and most structures use the same numbers, so residues can be compared directly. But not always: 1CDW numbers TBP 4 lower than UniProt (its Ser155 is UniProt's Ser159). The DBREF line at the top of a PDB file shows how the two numbering schemes line up.

In which tissues and cells is TLR4 most highly expressed? Why does this make sense for its function?

Highly expressed in placenta, spleen and peripheral blood leukocytes; detected in monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells and some T cells. These are innate immune cells that must detect invading bacteria quickly.

DERP2 is listed as an interaction partner. What is it, and why might it bind TLR4?

Der p 2, a major allergen of the house-dust mite (Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus). It resembles MD-2 in structure and can stand in for it, helping to activate TLR4 – which helps explain why it is such a potent allergen in asthma (Trompette et al., 2009).

How many leucine-rich repeats (LRRs) are annotated?

18 LRRs (residues 55–565), followed by an LRR C-terminal cap (LRRCT, 579–629). The repeats stack to form the curved ectodomain you will see in 3D.

Which domain is in the cytoplasmic part of TLR4?

The TIR (Toll/interleukin-1 receptor) domain, residues 672–815. TIR domains bind the TIR domains of adaptors such as MYD88 and TIRAP.

How many amino acids long is the canonical sequence?

TBP contains two similar repeated regions. What evolutionary event probably produced them?

A gene duplication: part of an ancestral gene was copied and the two copies fused, so the two halves of TBP's core have similar sequences. In 3D the two repeats fold into the two similar halves of the saddle-shaped core that sits astride the DNA (see 1CDW in the PDB chapter).

What is the accession of human TBP?

P20226 (TBP_HUMAN).

Which amino acid does the long run of Qs represent?

Glutamine (Gln, Q). The canonical sequence has 38 in a row (residues 58–95). Expansion of this poly-Q tract to about 47–63 repeats causes spinocerebellar ataxia 17 (SCA17) – see Disease & Variants.

In the Structure section, which chains of PDB entry 1C9B are TBP?

Chains B, F, J, N and R (residues 158–337). 1C9B contains five copies of a TBP–TFIIB–DNA complex. By contrast 1CDW has a single TBP (chain A) bound to DNA.

The Protein Data Bank: experimental structures

≈ 10 minWorkbench: PDB tab

The wwPDB is the worldwide archive of experimentally determined structures of proteins, nucleic acids and their complexes. It has three sites with different tools – RCSB PDB (USA), PDBe (UK) and PDBj (Japan) – but they share the same data. Each entry has a four-character ID such as 3FXI (upper or lower case).

Things to check for every entry
  • Method – X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) or NMR.
  • Resolution (X-ray and cryo-EM) – a smaller number means finer detail. At ~1.5 Å individual atoms are clear; at ~3 Å you can trace the chain and most side chains; beyond ~4 Å mainly the overall shape.
  • What was actually studied – structures are experiments, so they often contain only a fragment of the protein, engineered mutations or fusions, and molecules added to help crystallisation.

How many copies of TLR4 and of MD-2 (gene name LY96) does 3FXI contain? What does this tell you about the activated receptor?

Two of each: TLR4 = chains A, B; MD-2 (lymphocyte antigen 96) = chains C, D. LPS binding brings two TLR4–MD-2 units together into an “m”-shaped 2:2:2 complex. In the cell this dimerisation brings the two cytoplasmic TIR domains together, creating a platform for the adaptors MyD88 and TIRAP.

LPS appears in pieces: fatty acids (FTT, MYR, DAO), phosphate (PO4) and a branched sugar chain. Why might one ligand be split like this in a PDB entry?

Every chemical building block in the PDB is described by an entry in its Chemical Component Dictionary. A large, branched molecule like LPS (lipid A + core oligosaccharide) is therefore modelled as several components – glucosamines, phosphates, fatty-acyl chains, Kdo and heptose sugars. Parts that are disordered (e.g. the outer sugars) are not seen at all.

2.2 It's just text!

A structure file is a text file listing every atom with its x, y, z coordinates. The older .pdb format is read by most programs; the newer .cif (mmCIF) format is needed for very large structures.

Activity 2.2

What is the difference between ATOM and HETATM lines?

ATOM lines are standard amino acids or nucleotides in a polymer chain. HETATM (“hetero atom”) lines are everything else: ligands, ions, water, sugars and modified residues.

Why is searching with the UniProt accession more reliable than searching with a name or free text?

Names are ambiguous: a name search also finds other species (e.g. mouse TLR4) and related proteins, and free text matches any mention in titles or papers. The accession identifies exactly one protein sequence, and every PDB chain is mapped to UniProt (by the SIFTS project), so the search returns exactly the entries containing human TLR4 – 15 at the time of writing.

What method was used for the newest human TLR4 structures, and what did they study?

Cryo-EM: 8WO1 (2.24 Å) and 8WTA (2.9 Å), released in 2024, and 9J03 (2.7 Å, 2025) show human TLR4–MD-2 bound to synthetic lipid A mimetics. Cryo-EM now reaches resolutions that used to need crystals, and is well suited to large membrane-protein complexes. (Newer entries may have appeared since this was written.)

Most X-ray structures of TBP contain only residues ~159–339. Why do you think researchers crystallised just this part?

Residues ~159–339 form the conserved C-terminal core that binds the TATA box. The N-terminal region (with the poly-Q tract) is flexible and disordered, which stops proteins crystallising, so it is usually cut off. Cutting long proteins into stable pieces is a very common strategy – so a complete picture may need several entries (or a prediction).

Why is it important that LPS brings two TLR4–MD-2 units together?

Dimerisation is the activation step: it brings the two cytoplasmic TIR domains close together (they are not in the crystal), creating a platform that recruits the TIR-containing adaptors (TIRAP/MyD88, TRAM/TRIF) and starts signalling to NF-κB. Each LPS directly bridges the two units.

Which secondary structure dominates TLR4, and where is it?

β-strands. Each leucine-rich repeat contributes one short strand, and together they form a continuous parallel β-sheet lining the concave (inner) face of the curved “horseshoe”. The convex outer face is mostly loops with a few short helices. MD-2 binds on the concave side.

What kind of residues line MD-2's pocket, and why does that suit LPS?

Mainly hydrophobic residues (orange: Ile, Leu, Phe, Val…). They pack against the fatty-acyl chains of lipid A, which are hydrophobic. The polar phosphates and sugars stay at the mouth of the pocket, near polar and positively charged residues.

3.6 The LPS binding site

Pharmacology link

How a ligand binds – which residues it touches, and whether through hydrogen bonds, salt bridges or hydrophobic contacts – is the starting point for designing drugs that mimic or block it.

Activity 3.6 · Binding-site analysis
  1. Click LPS in the Workspace to select it. In the press Binding site. Residues within 4.5 Å appear as sticks, and dashed lines show hydrogen bonds and salt bridges.
  2. Hover over a dashed line to read which atoms it joins. Use the list of residue buttons to jump between them.
  3. Select three residues that contact the phosphates or sugars (click them) and press Label residues in the .
Which part of LPS is buried inside MD-2, and which parts contact TLR4?

Five of the six fatty-acyl (lipid) chains of lipid A are buried in MD-2's hydrophobic pocket. The sixth chain sticks out of MD-2 and packs against conserved phenylalanines of the second TLR4 (TLR4*), and the phosphates and core sugars form ionic and hydrogen-bond contacts with positively charged and polar residues of TLR4 and MD-2 (Park et al., 2009). This is how LPS bridges the two units.

List three residues that make polar contacts (hydrogen bonds or salt bridges) with LPS, with their chain.

For example: TLR4 (chain A) Arg264, Asp294, Tyr296, Lys341; MD-2 (chain C) Ser118, Ser120, Lys122. Across the dimer interface, TLR4* (chain B) Gln436 hydrogen-bonds the exposed acyl chain of LPS. Exact lists depend on the distance cut-off used.

Compare eritoran (magenta) with lipid A. How many lipid chains does each have, and why might eritoran block TLR4 rather than activate it?

Eritoran has four lipid chains, all buried in MD-2's pocket. E. coli lipid A has six: the extra chains push the phosphorylated glucosamine backbone ~5 Å out towards the solvent, so one lipid chain and the phosphates can contact the second TLR4 and drive dimerisation. Eritoran fills the pocket (competing with LPS) but cannot make this bridge, so no dimer forms and there is no signal – it is an antagonist.

Are Asp299 and Thr399 close to LPS? What might this suggest about how they reduce the LPS response?

No. Asp299 is about 14 Å from LPS, and Thr399 about 15 Å from LPS* (the other unit's LPS), so neither touches a ligand directly. The crystal structure of the variant receptor (4G8A; Ohto et al., 2012) still forms the normal “m”-shaped complex with LPS, with only local changes near residue 299 that alter the surface of TLR4. Their effect is therefore subtle, not a blocked binding site.

AlphaFold: predicted structures

≈ 15 minWorkbench: 3D viewer

AlphaFold2 (Google DeepMind) predicts a protein's 3D structure from its sequence. It is a neural network trained on structures from the PDB and on evolutionarily related sequences from UniProt – so its success rests on fifty years of experimental work. The AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (alphafold.ebi.ac.uk, run with EMBL-EBI) holds predictions for over 214 million proteins.

Two confidence scores
  • pLDDT (0–100) – confidence in each residue's local structure. Dark blue > 90 very high · light blue 70–90 confident · yellow 50–70 low · orange < 50 very low (often disordered).
  • PAE (predicted aligned error, in Å) – confidence in the relative position of two residues. Low PAE between two domains means their arrangement is reliable; high PAE means it is not.

A prediction is a model, not an experiment: AlphaFold DB models are single chains, without partners, ligands, ions or sugars.

4.1 The TLR4 model

Activity 4.1
  1. Load the AlphaFold model of TLR4: type O00206 in the AlphaFold box of the viewer and press Load model.
  2. The model is coloured by pLDDT, and the AlphaFold confidence panel (top right) summarises it. Open pLDDT along the sequence in the panel.

Need the model file for other software (ICM-Browser, PyMOL, ChimeraX…)? Use the .pdb file button in the , or download it from the model's page on AlphaFold DB. UniProt's Structure section links to the same page.

What is the mean pLDDT of the model?

About 89 (89.2). Around 71% of residues are “very high” confidence.

Which regions have very low confidence (orange)? Suggest a reason for each.

The N-terminus (≈1–25) – the signal peptide, which is cleaved off and has no fixed structure; and the C-terminal tail (≈821–839), which is flexible. The linker between the membrane helix and the TIR domain (≈655–672) is also low. Very low pLDDT usually indicates flexible or intrinsically disordered regions rather than a “wrong” structure.

4.2 A complete receptor – almost

Activity 4.2
  1. Rotate the model: you can see the LRR ectodomain, the single transmembrane helix and the cytoplasmic TIR domain – everything in one chain.
  2. In the UniProt entry, open Family & Domains and click the TIR domain to highlight it on the model.
  3. Resatorvid (TAK-242) binds Cys747. Find it: Hover over it to read its pLDDT.
The TIR domain has high confidence, but look at where it sits relative to the ectodomain. Could it be arranged like that in a cell?

No. In a cell the ectodomain is outside and the TIR domain is in the cytoplasm, on opposite sides of the membrane, with the helix crossing the ~4 nm lipid bilayer. AlphaFold has no membrane, so the relative orientation of the domains is essentially arbitrary – the PAE plot (next) confirms that it is not predicted with any confidence.

4.3 The PAE plot

Activity 4.3
  1. In the confidence panel, open Predicted aligned error (PAE) plot. Dark green = low expected error; white = high.
  2. Two dark squares lie along the diagonal – the ectodomain and the TIR domain. Drag a box over the pale area between them (x ≈ 670–815, y ≈ 30–620). The residues are selected and the mean PAE is shown.
What does the PAE tell you about how the ectodomain and TIR domain are arranged in this model?

The expected error between them is about 27 Å – close to the maximum (~32 Å) – so AlphaFold cannot tell how the two domains sit relative to each other. Within each domain the PAE is low (~5 Å), so each fold is reliable on its own. Interpret domains separately, not their arrangement.

4.4 Prediction versus experiment

Activity 4.4
  1. Make sure 3FXI is loaded too.
  2. Superpose the model on the crystal structure – press Superpose onto… in the confidence panel, or
  3. Colour 3FXI chain A grey and hide chains B and D, to compare.
What RMSD did you get (in Å)?

About 0.7 Å over ~590 Cα atoms – an excellent match (differences below ~1–2 Å are similar to the differences between two crystal structures of the same protein). The prediction of the ectodomain is essentially as good as the experiment. One caveat: 3FXI (released in 2009) was among the structures AlphaFold2 was trained on, so this is not a fully independent test.

What does the AlphaFold model not show that 3FXI does? Why does this matter when thinking about drugs?

No MD-2 partner, no LPS (or any ligand), no second TLR4 (the dimer), no glycans, ions or water. The LPS-binding pocket is in MD-2, so a drug-design project using only the TLR4 model would miss the binding site. Ligands and partners can also change a protein's shape. Complexes need experiments or newer tools that model complexes and ligands (e.g. AlphaFold3).

Extension: TBP Extension

Activity 4.5
  1. Load the AlphaFold model of TBP: Compare its confidence with the TBP coverage map from Chapter 2.
Which part of TBP is predicted confidently, and which is not? How does this match the experimental structures?

The C-terminal core (≈160–339), which binds DNA, has very high confidence (mean pLDDT ≈ 96) – the same part seen in the X-ray structures. Most of the N-terminal region (≈1–57 and 96–159) has low or very low pLDDT: it is disordered, which is why it is cut off before crystallisation.

The poly-Q tract (58–95) is the surprise: AlphaFold builds it as one long helix with “confident” pLDDT (≈ 80). But the PAE between this helix and the core is ≈ 28 Å, so its position relative to the core is unknown, and no experimental structure shows the helix. Treat confident-looking helices in repetitive sequence with caution.

Make a labelled figure

≈ 10 minWorkbench: 3D viewer

Your coursework asks for a 3D view of your receptor, labelled, with a clear colour code and key. Labels help a reader understand the picture without having to read the legend. Here you will recreate the classic picture of the TLR4–MD-2–LPS dimer.

What makes a good molecular figure
  • One message. Decide what the picture should show (the overall fold? the binding site?) and hide everything else – extra copies, water, clutter.
  • Few, meaningful colours – e.g. one colour per molecule – and a key that explains every colour.
  • Labels on the important parts only, with leader lines where needed.
  • Orientation that shows the feature clearly (save it as a view).
  • White background and high resolution for reports (2× or more).
  • A figure legend that gives the PDB ID and reference, what each colour means, and the software used.
Activity 5.1 · Build the figure
  1. Start afresh with 3FXI.
  2. Give each molecule its own colour: select a chain in the Workspace and click a colour in the . Show LPS and LPS* as sticks.
  3. Rotate until you can see the “m” shape clearly – a side view with the ends that lead into the membrane at the bottom – then save it in Views.
  4. In , press Colour key. Double-click the entries to rename them (e.g. “TLR4”, “TLR4*”). Drag the key to a corner.
  5. Add 3D labels: select each molecule and press 3D text… (or Label residues) – TLR4, MD-2, LPS and the starred copies.
  6. Add a 2D title.
  7. In , download a PNG at 2× (or copy it straight into Word or PowerPoint).
  8. In Views, press Save session so you can carry on later (open it again with Open session).

Glossary

AccessionStable identifier of a UniProt entry, e.g. O00206
Swiss-Prot / TrEMBLReviewed (expert-curated) / unreviewed (automatically annotated) parts of UniProtKB
IsoformProtein variant produced from the same gene, e.g. by alternative splicing; one is chosen as “canonical”
PTMPost-translational modification, e.g. glycosylation, phosphorylation, disulfide bonds
ResolutionLevel of detail in an X-ray or cryo-EM structure, in Å – smaller is better
B-factorHow much an atom's position is smeared out (mobility/uncertainty) in an experimental structure
Asymmetric unitWhat was deposited: the smallest part of the crystal from which the whole unit cell can be generated by symmetry – may contain several copies
Biological assemblyThe form believed to exist in the cell (e.g. a monomer or a dimer)
pLDDTAlphaFold's per-residue confidence (0–100)
PAEPredicted aligned error: AlphaFold's confidence in the relative position of two residues (Å)
RMSDRoot-mean-square deviation between matched atoms after superposition (Å)
Hydrogen bond / salt bridgePolar interactions, typically 2.6–3.5 Å between heavy atoms; a salt bridge is between oppositely charged groups
